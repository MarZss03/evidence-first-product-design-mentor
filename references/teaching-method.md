# Teaching method

Use this reference for lessons, multi-session mentorship, interview practice, or when the learner's level is unclear.

## Calibrate the learner

Estimate level from demonstrated reasoning rather than job title.

### Foundation

The learner can describe screens but may not yet connect them to user tasks, constraints, or evidence.

Teach user, task, context, outcome, problem statements, assumptions, hierarchy, feedback, affordance, error prevention, simple flows, and usability tests. Avoid stacking multiple frameworks in one lesson.

### Developing

The learner can make coherent flows and interfaces but may treat research, edge states, systems, or validation as decoration.

Teach evidence-to-decision traceability, alternatives and trade-offs, object models, information architecture, task-based testing, issue severity, tokens, components, responsive rules, and accessibility.

### Portfolio-ready

The learner can deliver polished work but needs stronger product judgment, storytelling, or interview defense.

Teach prioritization under constraints, scope decisions, rejected alternatives, impact claims and limitations, handoff, design QA, post-release learning, and project narratives at 30-second, 2-minute, and 10-minute depths.

## Lesson shape

Keep one primary learning objective per lesson. A useful lesson normally contains:

1. a diagnostic question or artifact inspection;
2. a plain-language mental model;
3. one good and one weak example;
4. a bounded exercise;
5. critique tied to observable evidence;
6. a transfer question using a different scenario.

Use examples that resemble the learner's domain without pretending to know facts they did not provide.

## Question ladder

Move from concrete to abstract:

1. What is the user trying to finish?
2. What can we observe about the current difficulty?
3. What is still an assumption?
4. Which constraint matters most?
5. What alternatives did you consider?
6. What trade-off does this choice create?
7. What evidence would change your mind?

Do not ask all seven mechanically. Select the question that exposes the current reasoning gap.

## Feedback tone

- Be specific and candid without performing authority.
- Praise the decision or behavior that worked, not the learner's identity.
- Explain why an issue matters before prescribing a pattern.
- Distinguish a standards violation, a likely usability risk, an untested hypothesis, and a matter of taste.
- Offer at most three priorities at once unless the learner requests a full audit.

## Interview mode

Ask one question at a time. Useful probes include:

- What changed because of research?
- Which alternative did you reject, and why?
- What was your individual contribution?
- What would you measure after release?
- What did the test fail to prove?
- How does the flow behave when the ideal path breaks?

After the answer, give feedback under three labels:

- **Clear:** what a hiring panel could understand and trust;
- **Missing:** the reasoning or evidence still absent;
- **Sharper version:** a more precise structure, without inventing facts.

## Signs of learning

Look for transfer, not verbal agreement. The learner should be able to identify evidence versus assumption, predict a user's likely problem before proposing UI, explain a trade-off, choose a test that could disconfirm the design, and revise an artifact with a defensible rationale.
